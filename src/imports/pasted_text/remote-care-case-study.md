PROFESSIONAL PORTFOLIO CASE STUDY
Remote Care Management Storyline & Design Specifications
A public-safe, narrative-forward case-study framework that emphasizes discovery leadership, program orchestration, systems thinking, and enterprise healthcare product strategy.

Recommended use: Revise the portfolio page so that the story is carried primarily by precise language and selective abstracted visuals, rather than by proprietary workflow imagery.


1. Strategic Reframe
The earlier visual portfolio concept leaned heavily on embedded workflow and prototype imagery. For a public or semi-public professional portfolio, the stronger and safer approach is to use the visuals sparingly and let the narrative prove the depth of the work.
The Remote Care Management story is compelling because it demonstrates leadership across discovery, clinical workflow synthesis, requirements definition, vendor/platform alignment, and program-level decision-making. It positions UX as a strategic operating discipline, not simply as interface design.


2. Recommended Public-Facing Portfolio Angle
Position this work as a case study in enterprise healthcare transformation. The page should not primarily say, “I designed a care-management interface.” It should say, “I led the discovery and program shaping needed to turn a fragmented, multi-stakeholder care-management opportunity into an actionable product and workflow strategy.”


3. Narrative Architecture for the Case Study Page
The portfolio page should unfold like a leadership story. Each section should answer a different executive question: What was the challenge? Why was it complex? What role did I play? How did I create clarity? What changed because of that work?


4. Recommended Hero Story
The hero should introduce the case study as a strategic leadership effort, not as a set of interface artifacts.


Suggested Hero Supporting Copy
I led UX-driven discovery and program definition for a Remote Care Management initiative focused on oncology care. The work required translating patient and care-team needs into a coherent product direction spanning symptom management, ePROs, triage, care planning, documentation, billing-aware workflows, and integration across internal and partner platforms.
My contribution went beyond interface design: I helped frame the problem, align stakeholders, decompose requirements, clarify phased scope, and shape end-to-end workflows that could support both clinical value and operational feasibility.
Metadata Block


5. The Challenge - Explain the Complexity Before the Solution
This section should establish that the work addressed a systemic care-delivery and operational problem. Use narrative language to show that the program sat at the intersection of patient experience, clinical execution, technical integration, and reimbursement-aware operations.




6. My Role - Make the Leadership Dimension Explicit
The case study should state plainly that this work required a blend of strategic UX leadership, discovery discipline, and program-management judgment.




7. Discovery Story - Emphasize How Clarity Was Created
This should be one of the strongest sections in the portfolio. The deck supports a credible story that the work was informed by practice-level realities, clinical personas, vendor collaboration, and cross-functional review. Because specific interview names are not documented, keep the language accurate and describe the participant groups and synthesis process rather than overstating named one-on-one interviews.
Suggested Public-Facing Section Header
Discovery: translating fragmented clinical realities into a shared program direction.


Discovery Copy Block
I led discovery to clarify how a Remote Care Management model could support oncology patients and care teams across a fragmented ecosystem. The work was informed by practice-level workflow realities, care-manager and nurse personas, product and clinical leadership perspectives, billing considerations, and direct alignment with partner-platform capabilities. Rather than treating these as separate inputs, I synthesized them into a single product narrative that distinguished patient needs, care-team tasks, system dependencies, and post-MVP growth areas.
This discovery work created a shared language for the program. It helped the team discuss not just what features might exist, but what care-management workflows had to be supported end-to-end, where operational burden would surface, which integrations were critical, and what gaps required explicit future planning.

8. Program Management Story - Show How the Work Moved Forward
The portfolio should explicitly describe program-management strength: not in the narrow sense of tracking tasks, but in shaping a complex initiative so that teams could make decisions, sequence the work, and understand dependencies.




Program Management Copy Block
A central part of my contribution was program orchestration. I led the work of translating a broad care-management ambition into a more concrete delivery frame: what capabilities mattered, which workflows had to be connected, what dependencies could block implementation, and where phased planning was necessary. This helped the organization move from a collection of related ideas toward a more unified product and operational strategy.
The case study should make clear that the design work was not downstream of the program. UX actively shaped the program by defining workflows, clarifying requirements, aligning stakeholders, and reducing decision risk.
9. Turning Discovery into Product Direction
This section should show your ability to convert insight into structure. Use a simple, text-based lifecycle rather than proprietary diagrams.


Recommended Narrative
I defined the care-management experience as an end-to-end operational flow rather than a disconnected set of screens. The work linked program eligibility, consent, enrollment, recurring patient-reported outcomes, triage, documentation, and billing-aware reporting into a coherent lifecycle. This made the program easier to reason about, easier to discuss across disciplines, and more actionable for future design and delivery planning.
10. Collaboration Story - Make Orchestration Visible
The deck supports a strong collaboration story. The portfolio should make clear that the initiative depended on aligning multiple domains and that you played a connective role across them.




11. Outcomes - Describe the Strategic Value Without Overclaiming
Because the deck primarily documents program definition and execution leadership rather than quantified production metrics, the portfolio should avoid unsupported performance claims. Instead, emphasize the concrete strategic outcomes that the work produced.


Recommended Outcome Copy
The work helped transform a high-level Remote Care Management ambition into a clearer program direction. By connecting discovery, workflow modeling, requirements definition, technical constraints, and delivery phasing, I helped the organization evaluate what a scalable oncology care-management model would require and where the most important strategic decisions needed to be made.
12. Minimal-Image Strategy for a Proprietary Portfolio
Use images only when they add unique explanatory value and can be safely shared. The public portfolio can be excellent with little or no proprietary imagery if the page is structured well.


13. Web/Page Design Specifications for a Narrative-Forward Case Study
The page design should create confidence through hierarchy and rhythm, not through volume of imagery.


14. Recommended Case Study Copy - Complete Narrative Draft
The following text can serve as the foundation for the actual portfolio page.
Case Study Title


Intro Paragraph
I led UX-driven discovery and program definition for a Remote Care Management initiative designed to strengthen oncology patient support and care-team coordination. The opportunity spanned patient education, symptom reporting, ePROs, triage, care planning, documentation, reimbursement-aware workflows, and integration across internal and partner platforms.
Challenge Paragraph
The challenge was not simply to design an interface. It was to translate a fragmented and multi-stakeholder care-management problem into a shared product direction that could account for clinical realities, operational burden, data and integration needs, partner delivery constraints, and future scalability.
My Contribution Paragraph
My work focused on creating clarity where the program was complex. I led discovery conversations, synthesized workflow and stakeholder inputs, defined end-to-end care-management journeys, decomposed requirements into more actionable program areas, and helped align product, engineering, clinical, billing, and vendor perspectives. This positioned UX as a requirements-defining and decision-support discipline, not a downstream production function.
Discovery Paragraph
The discovery process drew on practice-informed workflow pain points, care-manager and nurse personas, cross-functional stakeholder input, and vendor alignment conversations. I translated these inputs into a clearer model of the program: what patients needed, what care teams needed to act on, which capabilities required enterprise integration, and where operational or reimbursement concerns needed to influence the design.
Program Management Paragraph
I also helped shape the execution path. The work involved clarifying MVP and post-MVP considerations, highlighting dependencies such as platform embedding, data exchange, SSO, and reporting, and framing delivery discussions so that the organization could evaluate feasibility alongside user and business value.
Outcome Paragraph
The result was a more structured Remote Care Management program direction: one that connected patient engagement, clinical operations, workflow design, technical feasibility, and business accountability into a coherent strategy. The case study demonstrates my ability to lead discovery in ambiguous environments, orchestrate complex programs, and turn enterprise healthcare complexity into actionable product direction.

Revise the portfolio page so that the primary artifact is the story itself. Use language to demonstrate judgment, scope, leadership, and program impact. Use only a small number of generalized, non-proprietary visuals to support comprehension.
